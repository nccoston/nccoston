The crash

Since 1995 — before Google existed — a few hundred Michigan fans have argued about football on a message board that still looks the way the internet did then. Threaded replies, handles, an asterisk in the subject line when the subject is the whole post. Marriages, kids, jobs, and national championships all got announced there. One member found it via Ask Jeeves. Another wrote a book about it.

In the summer of 2026 the free service hosting it crashed and never came back. No warning, no export, no support — the host's own help forum had been dead for years. Thirty years of community suddenly had no address.

Thirty years, five homes

The community predates its every address. It began on a fan-run recruiting board in 1995, moved to the beloved “VV board” in 1996 (famous for showing whole posts without a click), became The Victors around 1999, and rode a free 1998-era host for decades after that — through outages, ads, and neglect — because moving felt impossible.

The 2026 crash forced the question. The answer, this time, was an address the community owns. When the new board ran a roll call, members dated themselves to every era back to 1995 — and the founder of the 1996 board showed up in the thread, to a chorus of “long time no see.”

The rebuild

Nick Coston — not a developer, not the admin, not even an elder; a member since 2017 and one of the board's youngest — rebuilt it in six evenings, directing AI tools (Claude Code) the way an architect directs a construction crew: deciding what to build, reviewing everything against the community's thirty years of instinct, and testing on the toughest QA department in existence — the members themselves, live. {{ days_live }} days and {{ changes }} shipped changes later, he still can't read the code. He can tell you what every line is for.

The admin title came afterward, the old-fashioned way: he built the place, so he holds the keys.

The design brief was one sentence: keep everything the community loved, fix everything the old host wouldn't. The 1998 look stayed. The threads, handles, and asterisk culture stayed. Underneath: photo and video posting, polls, score-prediction games with a leaderboard, a live college scoreboard, an ephemeral chat room, a member-voted Hall of Fame, cross-device read tracking, moderation tools, privacy-preserving analytics, and nightly tested backups — so no crash can ever take the community's history again.

Six days, then a season

  • Day 0 — The old host dies. The community waits around a dead URL.
  • Day 3 — A clean rebuild goes live on its own address. Members re-register by the dozens the first night.
  • Week 1 — Photos, polls, search, edit, dark mode; the member feature-request firehose opens and never closes.
  • Week 2 — Pick 'em games, Hall of Fame, live chat, video clips; the community passes its old size.
  • Week 3 — Live scoreboards, game-day mode, a weekly podcast integration, tested disaster recovery. 350 members, ~35,000 pageviews a day.
  • Week 5 — The season opens. The first football Saturday finds the ceilings: the server hits its memory limit under real load, and the month's bandwidth allowance goes in six days. Both fixed inside the weekend.
  • Week 6 — Two games, built from nothing and put on the board. A member asks for a countdown clock at one in the morning and has one by breakfast.
  • Week 10 — The board becomes an MCP server. Any AI assistant can read it, search it, and follow the archive in order; nothing a logged-out reader can't already see.
  • Week 11 — Polls become threads. Chat learns to fill a phone screen, which is where most of the members live.
  • Week 12 — Live polls in the room, then live voice: an admin opens it, whoever talks reaches everyone, nothing to find and nothing recorded. A hit on its first night.
  • Week 14 — The offensive coordinator is fired on a Sunday afternoon. The voice room holds hundreds of members that day, most of them on phones, screens off.
  • Week 15 — A member asks for a timeline of every red flag the board called in real time. The board reads its own archive through its own MCP server, 29,000 posts, and publishes the receipts by Sunday.

Live voice, on phones

The request was one sentence: hands-free, zero-lift voice chat. Open the room, hear the room.

Listening is automatic. If someone is talking when you open chat, you hear them, the way you'd hear a room you walked into. Talking is the opt-in: one tap, and a mute and a leave. The board brokers sessions with Cloudflare's real-time relay and never touches the audio; nothing is recorded, and the room closes itself when it has been empty two hours.

Every rule in it was written by a phone. The first night, a talker on an iPhone could not be heard, so the microphone got a connection of its own. An admin's phone dropped and closed the room on thirty people, so the room learned to stay open until an admin closes it. A member's car Bluetooth handed the board a silent microphone, so the board now watches the mic and says so. The screen stays on while you're in the room, because a phone that sleeps hangs up.

The build took an evening. The next three were spent listening to the people using it.

A board any assistant can read

Point an AI assistant at /mcp and it can search the board, read a thread, follow the archive in order, and look up the standings. The server is read-only in a way that can be tested rather than promised: it opens the database in read-only mode, and the rule for what it may return is exactly what a logged-out browser can already see. The test suite seeds a board where every post carries the same address and every account the same password hash, then tries to reach them through every tool.

It paid for itself in week fifteen. When the season went bad and a member asked for a timeline of every red flag the board had called in real time, the board read its own ten game weekends through its own server, about 29,000 posts, and published the receipts: {{ receipts_n }} of them, each one a quote, a poster, a timestamp, and a link to the post as written. The first body-language call was 8:37 PM on opening night.

Game day

A football board is only as good as it is on a Saturday.

The Scores board carries a live scoreboard for all of college football — every game in the top division, not just Michigan's, refreshed from the wire and with rules about when a game is finished enough to leave the page. On game day the whole board changes: a banner, a waving flag, the marching band's pregame one tap away.

The week's pick 'em thread is posted by Skeeps, a reserved account that is not a person. It goes up on Monday so there is a week to think about it, locks itself at kickoff, counts the picks — including the ones left the old-fashioned way, as replies — and posts the standings when the game is over.

The scoring rule was not designed. It was remembered: closest to the final total, win or lose, which is how this board has always done it. A member corrected the first version from memory and the correction shipped that afternoon. The board also tracks Slippery Rock, a Division II school in Pennsylvania, for reasons every Michigan fan understands and nobody else ever will.

The arcade

Six weeks in, the members stopped asking for features and started asking for toys.

So the board grew two games of its own. Victard Bowl is a pixel football game — Michigan's real schedule, twelve weeks, you call the plays and you can take the field on defense too. Stadium Guesser drops you in front of a college football stadium and asks you to find it on a map of the lower 48; 135 of them, scored on how many miles you were off.

Neither one phones anywhere. The football game is a canvas and a few hundred lines of JavaScript. The map is not a mapping service — it is public-domain census outlines projected once at build time, so there is no key to buy, no bill to run up, and nothing to break when somebody else's service changes. Both keep leaderboards on the board itself, and the house robot posts the standings to the Scores page once a month.

Incident log

Real communities generate real production incidents. A selection, each diagnosed and fixed in hours — usually before most members noticed:

Photos posting sideways

Phone cameras store orientation as metadata; the upload compressor was stripping it without applying it.

Fix: apply orientation before resizing. Every phone photo since lands right-side up.

A password reset that didn't stop anyone

Year-long login sessions meant a reset password couldn't kick a problem user's existing logins.

Fix: per-account session tokens, rotated on reset — every live session dies instantly, on every device.

3,900 daily "visitors" on a 350-member board

The traffic counter was counting the CDN's relay addresses as people.

Fix: read the real client address from the CDN header. Honest numbers overnight; the analytics stayed anonymous throughout.

Chat messages appearing twice

Two overlapping refresh requests could each draw the same new messages — a classic race, surfacing only when the room got busy.

Fix: client-side dedupe by message id, one refresh in flight at a time.

The backup that would have hung the board at midnight

The first nightly-snapshot implementation ran inside an open database transaction — a deadlock that would fire on the first pageview of every new day.

Fix: caught before it ever shipped, by an actual restore drill. Untested backups are hope; these are tested.

A 48-megapixel phone photo against a 512MB server

Modern phone cameras produce files that decode to roughly 150MB of raw pixels. Two members uploading at once on game day was enough to walk the instance into its memory ceiling and restart it.

Fix: decode at reduced scale rather than full size, rotate after shrinking instead of before, and allow only two decodes at a time. The ceiling stopped being reachable.

Four Memorial Stadiums

Illinois and Indiana were both handed a photograph of Nebraska's stadium. Four schools call their ground Memorial Stadium, and the lookup was keyed by the stadium's name — so all four shared one slot and overwrote each other.

Fix: key by the school, and stop trusting names altogether. Every photograph is now checked against the stadium's real coordinates and rejected if it sits more than 25 miles away. Champaign to Lincoln is 450.

The voice room that closed on thirty people

Voice stayed open only while an admin was connected. An admin's phone dropped its connection and the room ended for everyone in it.

Fix: the room is open once an admin opens it, and stays open until an admin closes it or it sits empty for two hours. The admin's connection is a connection, not a key.

The talker nobody could hear

Listening and talking shared one connection to the relay. On an iPhone, adding the microphone to a connection already pulling the room renegotiated it into silence.

Fix: the microphone gets a session of its own. The board also watches the outgoing audio and tells the talker, in words, when the phone has quietly muted them.

The podcast box that vanished at midnight

The week's episode showed on the homepage on Wednesdays. One week it went up at 9:16 PM on Wednesday, so the box appeared for two hours and forty-four minutes and was gone by morning. Some weeks the show is recorded on a Thursday, and those never showed at all.

Fix: the box follows the episode, not the calendar: two days from upload, any day of the week, and it stays through a bad refresh instead of blanking.

The stack

One Python file — {{ "{:,}".format(loc) }} lines as of this page load — plus SQLite, plain HTML templates, and one small CSS file. No framework churn, no build step, no JavaScript beyond what the chat, the voice room and the scoreboard genuinely need. It runs on a single $7/month server with a persistent disk, behind a CDN, deploying automatically on every push. Voice rides Cloudflare's real-time relay; scores come from ESPN; the rest is the one file.

The code was written by AI (Claude Code) under direction; every feature was specified, reviewed, and accepted by a human who knows the community — then tested by {{ members }} people who don't file bug reports so much as post them publicly with jokes attached. Feature requests routinely shipped the same afternoon they were posted, which the members found funnier than the features. Every change is in a public running log on the board, {{ changes }} entries as of this page load, and each ships with the test that proves it.

The lesson

The engineering turned out to be the easy half. The hard half was twenty-five years in the making: knowing what a community needs, what it will reject, and when to say no — then saying yes fast to everything else.

AI didn't replace a developer here. It let a community person become the architect — social and engineering, both halves.

Fifteen weeks in, the members have stopped being surprised that things ship. The surprise now runs the other way: a request is posted at night as a joke, and in the morning it is a page on the board. That is the part of this story that is not about software.
